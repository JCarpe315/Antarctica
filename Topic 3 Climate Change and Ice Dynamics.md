# PART ONE: THE STATE OF THE ICE

---

## 1. Antarctica in Context

Antarctica is a continent roughly one and a half times the size of the United States, buried under an ice sheet that averages more than two kilometers thick. It holds about 90 percent of all the ice on Earth and about 70 percent of all freshwater. If every bit of that ice melted, global sea levels would rise by roughly 58 meters — about 190 feet. That will not happen in our lifetimes, or even our grandchildren's lifetimes. But the ice does not need to melt entirely to cause serious problems. Even a small fraction of it, added to the oceans, would reshape coastlines worldwide.

For decades, Antarctica was considered stable — too cold, too isolated, too vast to respond quickly to a warming world. That assumption has changed. Over the past twenty years, evidence has accumulated that parts of Antarctica are already responding, and in ways that were not fully anticipated.

---

## 2. How Scientists Measure Ice Loss

Before diving into the numbers, it helps to understand how scientists know what is happening to the ice. There are three main approaches.

**Satellite gravimetry:** The GRACE and GRACE-FO missions are essentially scales in space. They measure tiny changes in Earth's gravity field. When ice melts or flows into the ocean, the mass of the ice sheet decreases, and gravity weakens slightly in that region. These satellites can detect changes equivalent to a few millimeters of water spread across an area the size of a continent.

**Satellite altimetry:** Missions like ICESat-2 and CryoSat-2 bounce laser or radar signals off the ice surface. By measuring how long the signal takes to return, scientists can track changes in ice surface elevation. If the surface is sinking, ice is being lost.

**Satellite interferometry:** Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites send radar waves that can penetrate clouds and darkness. By comparing images taken days or weeks apart, scientists can measure how fast the ice is moving — its velocity. Faster flow generally means more ice is being delivered to the ocean.

Each method has strengths and blind spots. GRACE cannot distinguish between ice loss and changes in snowfall or bedrock rebound. Altimetry struggles with areas of rough terrain. Interferometry measures speed but not thickness. That is why scientists combine all three, along with field measurements, to build a coherent picture.

## 3. The Numbers: How Much Ice Is Antarctica Losing?

### 3.1 The Long-Term Trend

The most comprehensive long-term assessment comes from the Ice Sheet Mass Balance Inter-comparison Exercise (IMBIE), an international effort to reconcile all available satellite data. Their 2023 assessment, covering 1992 to 2020, found that Antarctica lost approximately **2,670 billion metric tons of ice** over that 28-year period. The rate of loss was not steady — it **tripled** from the 1990s to the 2010s.

The GRACE and GRACE-FO satellites, which have been measuring since 2002, give a slightly different figure because they measure mass directly rather than volume. Their data suggests an average loss of roughly **135 billion metric tons per year** across the full 2002–2025 period. This contributes approximately **0.4 millimeters per year** to global sea level rise from Antarctica alone.

In 2024 specifically, the Copernicus Climate Change Service estimated **82 billion metric tons** of net mass loss. For the first eleven months of 2024, the State of the Climate report put the figure at **125 billion metric tons**.

These numbers are large, but they need context. One billion metric tons of ice, melted, would raise global sea levels by about 0.0028 millimeters. So 135 billion metric tons per year contributes roughly 0.4 millimeters per year to sea level rise. That sounds small, but it adds up. More importantly, the rate is increasing, and it comes on top of contributions from Greenland, mountain glaciers, and thermal expansion of seawater.

### 3.2 The 2021–2023 East Antarctic Anomaly

Between 2021 and 2023, something unusual happened in East Antarctica: the ice sheet actually gained mass. This was not because the ice sheet had stopped losing ice at its edges. It was because a series of powerful atmospheric rivers — narrow corridors of concentrated moisture — dumped enormous amounts of snow on East Antarctica. The GRACE satellites recorded this as a temporary mass increase.

This matters for two reasons. First, it shows that Antarctica's mass balance is a tug-of-war between input (snowfall) and output (ice flow, melt, and calving). Second, it shows that temporary gains do not reverse long-term trends. By 2024, the mass loss had resumed. May 2024 saw record-high snowfall across much of East and West Antarctica from atmospheric rivers, but September 2024 was the driest September on record. The net result for the year was still a loss.

### 3.3 Grounded Ice Lost

A 2026 study by Rignot and colleagues, using 30 years of satellite data, found that Antarctica lost **12,820 square kilometers of grounded ice** between 1992 and 2025. That is an area roughly the size of Connecticut, or an average of about 442 square kilometers per year. About 77 percent of Antarctica's coastline showed stable grounding lines during this period. But the 23 percent that retreated includes the fastest-flowing, most vulnerable glaciers.

## 4. "Greenlandification": A Possible Regime Shift

For most of the satellite era, scientists thought of Greenland and Antarctica as fundamentally different. Greenland sits at a lower latitude, experiences warmer summers, and loses ice through a combination of surface melt (warm air melts the ice from above) and outlet glacier retreat (warm ocean water melts ice from below, causing glaciers to speed up and break off into icebergs). Antarctica, being colder and more isolated, was thought to lose ice primarily through ocean-driven melting at its margins, with surface melt playing a smaller role.

In March 2026, a paper in *Nature Geoscience* by Mottram and colleagues introduced the term **"Greenlandification"** to describe a shift in Antarctica's behavior. The paper argued that Antarctica is increasingly exhibiting processes once thought to be primarily Greenlandic: faster outlet glacier retreat, longer melt seasons, and more surface melting in areas that historically stayed frozen year-round.

**Important caveat:** Our research found references to this paper and the term, but we did not retrieve the full paper text. The claims about its specific content are based on search snippets and secondary references. This is a recent, potentially contested framing, and the full scientific community response to it is not yet clear.

This does not mean Antarctica will melt as fast as Greenland (Greenland is smaller and at lower latitude). But it means the processes that drive rapid ice loss in Greenland — processes that scientists understand less well for Antarctica — are now active on the southern continent too.

## 5. Record Melting Events

### 5.1 December 2024: The All-Time Record

On December 25 and 26, 2024 — Christmas Day in the austral summer — Antarctica experienced its largest surface melt extent on record. More than **3 percent of the entire Antarctic ice sheet area** experienced surface melting. Surface temperatures across the ice sheet ran approximately 2 degrees Celsius above average during the second half of 2024.

To put this in perspective, the satellite record of Antarctic melt extends back to 1979. In that nearly half-century of data, no Christmas period had seen melting on this scale. Coastal areas that rarely experience melt saw widespread surface water.

### 5.2 June 2026: The Antarctic Peninsula Winter Heatwave

In June 2026 — the middle of the austral winter, when Antarctica should be at its coldest — the Antarctic Peninsula experienced a staggering heatwave. At Esperanza Base, the temperature reached **15.4 degrees Celsius**, breaking the previous June record of 13.3 degrees set in 1998. At Marambio Base, it hit **11.8 degrees Celsius**. At San Martín Base, **9.4 degrees Celsius**. These temperatures were roughly 20 degrees Celsius above the June average.

The heatwave caused approximately **650,000 square kilometers of sea ice to disappear** in the Bellingshausen Sea — an area larger than France. Winter heatwaves in Antarctica were virtually unheard of a decade ago. They are now happening with increasing frequency.

## 6. The Collapse of Antarctic Sea Ice

### 6.1 What Happened

Sea ice is frozen ocean water. It forms each winter around Antarctica and melts back each summer. It is distinct from the land ice sheet, though the two interact. From the 1970s through about 2015, Antarctic sea ice was actually expanding slightly — a puzzling trend that ran counter to the rapid decline of Arctic sea ice. Then, in 2016, it collapsed abruptly to record lows. Since then, it has remained at or near record lows every year. The 2024–2025 season saw the second-lowest maximum extent on record.

### 6.2 Why It Matters

Sea ice matters because it acts as a protective buffer for ice shelves — the floating extensions of the land ice sheet that hold back glaciers. Sea ice dampens ocean waves that would otherwise batter ice shelf fronts. It reflects sunlight (it has high albedo), keeping the region cooler. When sea ice disappears, ice shelves are exposed to more wave action, more sunlight absorption, and more warm ocean water.

The sea ice collapse is both a symptom of warming and a feedback mechanism that can accelerate ice shelf destruction.

### 6.3 The Stanford Study: Subsurface Heat

A study by researchers at Stanford University linked the collapse to subsurface ocean heat that had been trapped beneath the surface for years. **Salinity stratification** in the Southern Ocean — layers of water with different salt concentrations — had trapped relatively warm water below the surface. Increased storm activity caused **upwelling**, bringing this warm water to the surface where it melted the sea ice from below.

However, there is a puzzle here. While overall sea ice collapsed, some analyses of the Pacific sector (the Ross and Amundsen Seas) show complex and sometimes opposite trends. A 2025 paper in the *Journal of Climate* found that extreme sea ice events in the Ross Sea are driven by eddy meridional temperature advection and turbulent heat flux, with wind-driven mixing breaking stratification. The exact mechanism for why some Pacific sectors show cooling or ice gain while the continent overall loses sea ice remains unresolved.

## 7. The Major Glaciers: Where the Ice Is Going

### 7.1 The Grounding Line

To understand glacier retreat, one must understand the **grounding line**. This is the boundary where a glacier transitions from resting on bedrock to floating as an ice shelf. When warm ocean water melts an ice shelf from below, the ice thins. If it thins enough, the grounding line retreats inland. Once the grounding line moves into deeper water — which happens on a **retrograde slope**, where the bed dips downward inland — more ice is exposed to melting, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of retreat.

### 7.2 The 30-Year Record

A landmark 2026 paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* by Rignot and colleagues mapped grounding line retreat across Antarctica using 30 years of satellite data (1992–2025). The results are sobering:

| Glacier/Sector | Grounding Line Retreat (1992–2025) |
|---|---|
| **Smith Glacier** | **42 kilometers** |
| **Pine Island Glacier** | **33 kilometers** |
| **Thwaites Glacier** | **26 kilometers** |
| **Vanderford Glacier** | **26 kilometers** |
| **Pope Glacier** | **23 kilometers** |
| **Haynes Glacier** | **20 kilometers** |
| **Berry Glacier** | **18 kilometers** |
| **Hull Glacier** | **14 kilometers** |
| **Kohler Glacier** | **12 kilometers** |
| **East Getz** | **9 kilometers** |

Total grounded ice lost: **12,820 square kilometers** over 30 years. That is an average of roughly 442 square kilometers per year.

### 7.3 Thwaites Glacier: The Most Watched Glacier on Earth

Thwaites Glacier, often called the "Doomsday Glacier" in media coverage, is the most scrutinized glacier in Antarctica. It is roughly the size of Florida. Its bed slopes backward — as the grounding line retreats, it moves into deeper water, exposing more ice to melting from below. This is the definition of a self-reinforcing feedback.

The International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration (ITGC), a multi-national research program, has found that warm Circumpolar Deep Water is penetrating beneath the Thwaites Ice Shelf, melting it from below faster than many models predicted. In 2026, a study by Wells-Moran and colleagues in *PNAS* found that the **ice mélange** — a mixture of sea ice, icebergs, and snow that acts like a cork in a bottle, stabilizing the glacier's terminus — has experienced **near-total loss**. Without this buttressing, the glacier can flow faster into the ocean.

### 7.4 Pine Island Glacier

Pine Island Glacier, flowing into the Amundsen Sea, has retreated 33 kilometers since 1992. Like Thwaites, it has lost much of its protective mélange. It is one of the fastest-flowing glaciers in Antarctica, and its flow speed has increased over the past two decades.

### 7.5 East Antarctic Glaciers: The New Front

For years, East Antarctica was considered stable — too cold, too high, too far from warm ocean water. That assumption is crumbling.

- **Totten Glacier:** The largest glacier in East Antarctica. Its ice shelf is thinning. Warm water has been detected beneath it. Totten drains an area containing enough ice to raise global sea levels by **3.4 meters**.
- **Denman Glacier:** Has retreated 5–6 kilometers. It sits in the deepest land canyon on Earth below sea level, making it vulnerable to warm water intrusion.
- **Cook Glacier:** Its ice shelf collapsed in 2023, and the glacier has accelerated since.
- **Vanderford Glacier:** Retreated 26 kilometers — one of the largest retreats in East Antarctica.

### 7.6 The Antarctic Peninsula: Acceleration and Retreat

The Antarctic Peninsula has experienced some of the most dramatic changes. Following the collapse of the Larsen B Ice Shelf in 2002, glaciers in the region **doubled their flow speed**. More broadly, Antarctic Peninsula glaciers have shown **71 percent acceleration** in recent decades. This is not just a matter of individual glaciers retreating — it is a sector-wide increase in the rate at which ice is being delivered to the ocean.

### 7.7 The Northeast Antarctic Peninsula Mystery

Here is a genuine puzzle. The northeast Antarctic Peninsula (the Larsen A-B sector and the George VI Ice Shelf) has seen 2 to 18 kilometers of grounding line retreat in some areas. But Rignot and colleagues explicitly state that they do not have evidence for warm water intrusion in this sector. Something else is driving the retreat, but they do not know what.

Possible explanations being investigated include atmospheric rivers delivering warm, moist air; foehn winds (warm, dry downslope winds) causing surface melt and hydrofracture; or some combination of atmospheric and oceanic forcing. But as of 2026, **the mechanism is genuinely unknown**. This is not a case of scientists disagreeing about the cause. It is a case of scientists acknowledging that they do not yet know the cause.

## 8. Ice Shelf Dynamics: Collapse, Thinning, and Instability

### 8.1 What Ice Shelves Do

Ice shelves are the floating extensions of glaciers that extend out over the ocean. They do not contribute directly to sea level rise when they melt or break up, because they are already floating (like an ice cube in a glass of water). But they act as **buttresses** — they hold back the flow of land ice behind them. When an ice shelf collapses, the glaciers it was holding back accelerate dramatically. After the Larsen B Ice Shelf collapsed in 2002, glaciers in the region doubled their flow speed.

### 8.2 Ice Shelves That Have Collapsed

Since 1995, six major ice shelves have collapsed:

| Ice Shelf | Year(s) of Collapse | Area Lost |
|---|---|---|
| **Larsen A** | 1995 | ~2,000 square kilometers |
| **Larsen B** | 2002 | ~3,250 square kilometers |
| **Wilkins** | 2008–2009 | ~570 square kilometers |
| **Wordie** | 1980s–2009 | Gradual |
| **Jones** | 2000s | Gradual |
| **Müller** | 2000s | Gradual |

### 8.3 Ice Shelves Currently at Risk

- **Brunt Ice Shelf:** Calved iceberg A-81 in January 2023 (1,500 square kilometers). The remaining ice shelf accelerated from 900 meters per year to **1,500 meters per year** — a tenfold increase in its acceleration rate. It is now moving at roughly **4 meters per day**.
- **George VI Ice Shelf:** Thinning and retreating. Rignot found 2 to 6 kilometers of grounding line retreat with no clear warm-water explanation.
- **Amery Ice Shelf:** A 2025 study by Jin and colleagues found a **current reversal regime change** — warm water is now circulating beneath the ice shelf in a pattern that promotes melting.
- **Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf:** A 2025 study by Song and colleagues identified a potential tipping point. If warm water penetrates beneath this massive ice shelf — the second-largest in Antarctica — the cavity regime could shift to a persistently warm state.

### 8.4 The Reversibility Debate

This is one of the most important unresolved questions in Antarctic science: if warm water starts melting an ice shelf from below, can the process be reversed if the ocean cools again?

**The case for irreversibility:** Hellmer and colleagues (2017), Hazel and Stewart (2020), and Haid and colleagues (2023) found evidence of hysteresis in the Filchner-Ronne system — once warm water enters, ice-ocean feedbacks make it difficult to push back out. A 2024 standalone ice-sheet model by the TiPACCs project also showed irreversible retreat even if cold conditions were restored.

**The case for reversibility:** Reese and colleagues (2025) found a reversible transition for Filchner-Ronne. Caillet and colleagues (2023) found that Amundsen Sea transitions are reversible within **14 to 21 years** if the forcing is removed.

**The conditional view:** Saddier and colleagues (2025) concluded that both types of transition are possible, depending on the vertical mixing scheme at the ice-shelf front. They stated that improved representations of vertical mixing in ocean simulations are **"essential to reliably predict"** whether transitions are reversible.

**What this means:** We do not yet know whether some ice shelves are already past the point of no return. The answer depends on physics — specifically, how heat and salt mix at the boundary between ocean and ice — that scientists are still learning to model correctly.

### 8.5 Hydrofracture and the Firn Layer

**Hydrofracture** is the process that destroyed Larsen B in 2002. Meltwater ponds form on the ice shelf surface. The water fills cracks in the ice. Because water is denser than ice, the weight of the water forces the cracks open all the way through the ice shelf, causing it to shatter.

Whether hydrofracture occurs depends partly on the **firn layer** — the porous, compacted snow on top of the ice sheet. The firn acts like a sponge, absorbing meltwater. But as melting increases, the firn can become saturated. Once full, meltwater runs off instead of being absorbed, forming ponds that can trigger hydrofracture.

Projections suggest that Antarctic Peninsula ice shelves could lose **more than 50 percent of their firn air content** by 2100, even under the lowest-emissions scenario (SSP1-2.6). This makes them more vulnerable to hydrofracture.

### 8.6 Submesoscale "Ocean Storms"

A 2025 study by Poinelli and colleagues revealed a previously unknown mechanism beneath ice shelves. Small, intense ocean circulation features called **submesoscale eddies** — essentially "ocean storms" — can cause threefold increases in basal melt (melting from below) within hours during extreme events. These eddies account for roughly **one-fifth of the total variance in submarine melting**.

The process creates a feedback loop: melting creates unstable meltwater fronts, which intensify the eddies, which cause more melting. Most ice sheet models did not capture this mechanism until very recently.

## 9. The Hektoria Glacier Controversy

In March 2022, the Hektoria Glacier on the Antarctic Peninsula began retreating rapidly. By August 2023, it had retreated **23 to 25 kilometers** — the fastest retreat in the satellite era. At its peak, the glacier was calving icebergs at **800 meters per day**, ten times faster than previous records. By 2023, the glacier had thinned to 20 meters per year.

### 9.1 The Buoyancy-Driven Collapse Theory

In 2025, Ochwat and colleagues published a paper in *Nature Geoscience* proposing that Hektoria's retreat was driven by **buoyancy-driven collapse**. Their theory: the glacier's trunk thinned across a flat bedrock area until it went afloat. Once floating, it became unstable and produced runaway iceberg calving.

### 9.2 The Scientific Dispute

This explanation is actively contested by other leading researchers:

- **Anna Hogg** of the University of Leeds says her measurements suggest the ice in that sector was **"always fully floating."** If correct, buoyancy-driven collapse cannot explain the retreat, because the ice was never grounded there in the first place.
- **Christine Batchelor** of Newcastle University argues that if the ice was always floating, then the event is "much less unusual — essentially 'ice shelf calves icebergs.'"
- **Frazer Christie** of Airbus and the University of Cambridge notes there is **"significant disagreement"** about where Hektoria's grounding line actually was.

**Assessment:** The scientific community has not reached consensus on whether this was truly unprecedented grounded-ice retreat or a calving event mischaracterized by grounding line uncertainty. The dispute matters because if Hektoria's mechanism is real, similar glaciers could behave the same way.

## 10. What Lies Beneath: Subglacial Discoveries

### 10.1 Subglacial Lakes

Beneath the Antarctic ice sheet lies a hidden hydrological network. As of 2025, scientists have identified **681 subglacial lakes** in Antarctica out of 779 known globally. In 2025, Wilson and colleagues used CryoSat-2 radar altimetry to detect **85 new active lakes** — a 58 percent increase. These lakes show filling and draining cycles.

Five interconnected lake networks have been identified. Six lakes sit within 8 kilometers of the grounding line, with the closest just 4 kilometers away. The largest recorded drainage event was Cook West_67, which drained approximately **1.3 cubic kilometers** of water — the largest recorded subglacial lake drainage. The largest filling event was Lambert_84, which filled with approximately **2.5 cubic kilometers**. The median drainage time is **2.2 years**.

These drainage events matter because when a subglacial lake drains, the water can lubricate the interface between ice and bedrock, causing the overlying ice to speed up. **These processes are not currently accounted for in most ice sheet mass loss projections.**

### 10.2 Lake Vostok

Lake Vostok is the largest known subglacial lake — 15,690 square kilometers, roughly the size of Lake Ontario. It has been sealed beneath approximately 4 kilometers of ice for **14 to 15 million years**. Russian drillers penetrated the lake in 2012. The lake sits on a prominent linear magnetic anomaly whose origin is unknown.

### 10.3 Mercer Subglacial Lake

In 2025, the Subglacial Antarctic Lakes Scientific Access (SALSA) project drilled through 1,085 meters of ice into Mercer Subglacial Lake. They found entirely new microbial communities, genetically distinct from surface life. The team recovered **1,374 single-cell genomes**, most representing unknown species. These microbes survive without sunlight through **chemoautotrophy** — deriving energy from chemical reactions in the water — and heterotrophy — consuming organic matter.

### 10.4 Ancient Seawater Aquifer

In 2022, scientists discovered a reservoir of fossil seawater sealed beneath the Whillans Ice Stream. This water has been isolated from the ocean for millennia, providing a window into past ocean conditions.

### 10.5 Warm Caves Near Mount Erebus

Near the active volcano Mount Erebus, a network of steam-hollowed caves reaches temperatures up to **25 degrees Celsius (77 degrees Fahrenheit)**. DNA from algae, mosses, and animals has been found. The full extent of life in these caves is unknown.

### 10.6 East Antarctic Fan-Shaped Basin

In 2026, scientists discovered a massive interconnected basin structure in East Antarctica, linked to the breakup of the ancient supercontinent Gondwana and the separation of Antarctica from Australia.

## 11. The Atmosphere's Role

### 11.1 Atmospheric Rivers

Atmospheric rivers are narrow corridors of concentrated moisture in the atmosphere, sometimes called "rivers in the sky." When they hit Antarctica, they can cause both extreme snowfall and extreme melt.

- They occur roughly **3 days per year** at any given Antarctic location.
- They cause **50 to 70 percent** of extreme snowfall events in East Antarctica.
- They drive **40 to 80 percent** of total summer melt on West Antarctic ice shelves.
- A 2025 study by Maclennan and colleagues projected that atmospheric rivers will **double by 2100**.

Atmospheric rivers often couple with **foehn winds** on the Antarctic Peninsula, creating compound melt events. For example, in February 2022, an atmospheric river combined with foehn winds to produce record temperatures.

### 11.2 Foehn Winds

Foehn winds are warm, dry downslope winds that can raise temperatures dramatically. On James Ross Island, events in November 2022, December 2022, and January 2023 were driven by **isentropic drawdown** — a meteorological process where air descends and warms — producing **5 to 9.5 degrees Celsius** of warming.

### 11.3 The Southern Annular Mode

The Southern Annular Mode (SAM) describes the north-south movement of the westerly wind belt that circles Antarctica. A positive SAM means the winds contract toward the pole, potentially isolating Antarctica from warmer air masses but also driving stronger upwelling of warm deep water. A negative SAM means the winds expand northward, allowing more warm air to reach the continent. Ozone recovery is changing SAM behavior, with complex implications for Antarctic climate.

### 11.4 The Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation

The Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation (IPO) is a long-term climate pattern in the Pacific Ocean that affects Antarctic climate through teleconnections — atmospheric and oceanic links between distant regions. The IPO influences sea surface temperatures, which in turn affect atmospheric circulation patterns reaching Antarctica.

## 12. The Ocean's Role

### 12.1 Circumpolar Deep Water

Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) is a relatively warm water mass that circulates around Antarctica at depth. By "warm," scientists mean it is still near freezing — about 1 to 2 degrees Celsius — but that is enough to melt ice. When CDW reaches ice shelf cavities, it melts ice from below. In West Antarctica, CDW intrusion is the primary driver of ice shelf thinning and grounding line retreat. In East Antarctica, CDW is increasingly being detected beneath Totten, Denman, and other ice shelves.

### 12.2 Antarctic Bottom Water Slowdown

Antarctic Bottom Water (AABW) is the densest water mass in the global ocean. It forms when cold, salty surface water sinks, and it drives the deep branch of global ocean circulation. AABW has thinned by **more than 50 decibars per decade** since the mid-1980s, indicating that the overturning circulation connecting Antarctica to the global ocean is slowing.

### 12.3 Submesoscale Eddies

As described in Section 8.6, Poinelli and colleagues (2025) found that small-scale "ocean storms" beneath ice shelves can cause melt rates to spike by a factor of three within hours. These were previously invisible to most models.

## 13. Tipping Points and Committed Sea Level Rise

### 13.1 Winkelmann's 2026 Assessment

A major 2026 study in *Nature Climate Change* by Winkelmann and colleagues assessed **18 Antarctic basins** for tipping behavior. Their findings:

- Approximately **40 percent of West Antarctic ice** is already committed to eventual loss at current warming levels (about 1.3 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial temperatures).
- The **Amundsen Sea basin**, containing Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers, may already be past its tipping point.
- Ice loss in one basin can trigger feedbacks in connected basins, creating a cascading or domino effect.
- Each basin has a different threshold. Some require only slight additional warming; others are more stable.

### 13.2 What "Committed" Means

"Committed" sea level rise does not mean the water is in the ocean tomorrow. It means that the physical processes have been set in motion that will inevitably lead to a certain amount of ice loss, even if warming stopped today. The timescale ranges from decades to millennia depending on the glacier. The 12,820 square kilometers of grounded ice that crossed the grounding line between 1992 and 2025 is already committed — it will eventually melt or calve into icebergs.

## 14. Lessons from the Past: Ice Cores and Sediment Records

### 14.1 Vostok and EPICA Dome C

The Vostok and EPICA ice cores from East Antarctica provide a continuous record of past climate going back **800,000 years**. Over that entire period, atmospheric carbon dioxide ranged from **172 to 300 parts per million**. Today, CO2 is approximately **425 parts per million** — far above anything in that 800,000-year record.

### 14.2 Allan Hills Blue Ice

Discontinuous ice samples from the Allan Hills region extend back **2.7 million years**. They confirm that before 800,000 years ago — in what scientists call the "40,000-year world" because ice ages followed a 40,000-year cycle — CO2 variability was lower than it has been in the past 800,000 years.

### 14.3 Beyond EPICA

The Beyond EPICA project, currently drilling at Dome C North, reached **2,800 meters depth** as of 2026. Its target is a continuous record going back 1.2 to 2 million years, capturing the transition from the 40,000-year world to the 100,000-year world and helping scientists understand why CO2 became more variable.

### 14.4 SWAIS2C

In 2026, the SWAIS2C project drilled a **228-meter sediment core** from beneath **523 meters of ice** at Crary Ice Rise. This core contains **23 million years** of climate history. A key finding: evidence that the West Antarctic Ice Sheet retreated during past warm periods, leaving an ice-free ocean. If the West Antarctic Ice Sheet collapsed during past warm periods similar to today, it could collapse again.

## 15. The Hunga Tonga Eruption's Antarctic Legacy

The January 2022 eruption of Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai was the largest volcanic eruption since Krakatoa in 1883. It injected approximately **150 teragrams** (150 million metric tons) of water vapor into the stratosphere. Unlike volcanic ash, which falls out within months, stratospheric water vapor can persist for years — in this case, with an estimated residence time of about **9 years**.

The 2023 Antarctic polar vortex had the **largest volume of polar stratospheric clouds ever recorded** — approximately **55 million cubic kilometers**, compared to a typical average of about **20 million**. Polar stratospheric clouds catalyze ozone destruction. The eruption may have delayed ozone recovery over Antarctica. Because water vapor is a greenhouse gas, the eruption's effects on Antarctic climate are still being studied.

## 16. Why Money Matters for Antarctic Ice

Antarctic science is expensive. Operating a research station on the coldest, most remote continent on Earth requires ships, aircraft, specialized equipment, and enormous logistical support. The nations that fund this science gain influence over how Antarctica is governed. The companies that provide the logistics, the satellites, the ships, and the data gain contracts worth billions. The industries that harvest resources from the Southern Ocean — krill, fish, and potentially minerals in the future — have a financial stake in how the region is regulated.

This section maps the major financial and corporate actors with interests in Antarctica, how they are connected, and where conflicts of interest arise.

## 32. Scientific Gaps and Anomalies

### 32.1 The Northeast Antarctic Peninsula Mystery

The northeast Antarctic Peninsula has experienced 2 to 18 kilometers of grounding line retreat with **no evidence of warm water intrusion**.

Rignot et al. (2026): "We don't have evidence for warm water... Something else is acting — it's still a question mark."

**Status:** Unresolved. Atmospheric rivers and foehn winds are suspects but unconfirmed as primary drivers for this specific sector.

### 32.2 The Hektoria Glacier Dispute

Whether Hektoria's 23 to 25 kilometer retreat in 15 months represents truly unprecedented grounded-ice retreat or a calving event mischaracterized by grounding line uncertainty remains unresolved. Leading researchers actively disagree.

### 32.3 Ice-Shelf Cavity Reversibility

Whether warm water enters an ice shelf cavity and can ever be pushed back out is a fundamental physics question with major implications for sea level rise.

**The split:**
- Irreversible: Hellmer, Hazel & Stewart, Haid, TiPACCs (2024).
- Reversible: Reese (2025), Caillet (2023).
- Conditional: Saddier (2025) — depends on vertical mixing scheme.

**Status:** Answer determines whether some ice loss is already unstoppable.

### 32.4 Pacific-Sector Sea Ice Cooling Paradox

While overall Antarctic sea ice collapsed, some Pacific sector analyses show **opposite cooling trends**.

**Status:** The Stanford subsurface heat theory explains the overall collapse but not the regional patchiness. Mechanism unresolved.

### 32.5 Antarctic Bioalbedo Gap

Cryoconite holes and biological algae can reduce ice albedo by 48–56 percent (Di Mauro). But Antarctic-specific bioalbedo research is far less developed than Greenland's Black & Bloom project. The impact on Antarctic ice sheet mass balance is **largely unquantified**.

### 32.6 Geothermal Heat Flux Under Thwaites

Schroeder et al. (2014) found Thwaites minimum average heat flow ~114 milliwatts per square meter (vs. 65 continental average), with hotspots 200+ near Mount Takahe. **No new measurements have been published since 2014.** This "known unknown" could fundamentally alter ice loss projections but requires expensive deep-ice drilling.

### 32.7 Subglacial Lakes Not in Projections

Wilson et al. (2025) found 85 new active subglacial lakes, with drainage events that can lubricate ice flow. These are **not currently accounted for** in ice sheet mass loss models.

### 32.8 Cloud Radiative Effects

Antarctic cloud properties and their radiative impact on surface melt are **very poorly represented** in models. Limited recent literature found.

### 33.6 No Corruption Found

Despite extensive searching, **no corruption scandals, fraud cases, or financial misconduct in Antarctic research or logistics were found in the 2024–2026 period**. # PART FIVE: SUMMARY

---

## 35. What Is Known

1. **Antarctica is losing ice at an accelerating rate.** The current average is approximately 135 billion metric tons per year, contributing ~0.4 mm/year to sea level rise. The IMBIE assessment found the rate tripled from the 1990s to the 2010s.
2. **West Antarctica is the most vulnerable.** Thwaites, Pine Island, and the Amundsen Sea sector may already be past tipping points. Approximately 40% of West Antarctic ice is already committed to eventual loss.
3. **East Antarctica is no longer stable.** Totten, Denman, Cook, and Vanderford are retreating. The assumption of East Antarctic stability has crumbled.
4. **Sea ice has collapsed** and not recovered since 2016. After expanding from the 1970s through 2015, it abruptly declined and has remained at record lows.
5. **Record melting is now routine.** December 2024 and June 2026 both set records. June 2026 saw a mid-winter heatwave with temperatures 20°C above average.
6. **Antarctica may be "Greenlandifying."** A 2026 paper suggests Antarctica is exhibiting processes once associated with Greenland — faster outlet glacier retreat and longer melt seasons. (Note: full paper not retrieved; this is a recent, potentially contested framing.)
7. **Antarctic Peninsula glaciers have accelerated 71%.** Following ice shelf collapses, glaciers in the region have shown dramatic speed increases.
8. **Subglacial processes** (lakes, hydrology, geothermal heat) are active and largely unmodeled. 85 new active lakes were discovered in 2025; their drainage events are not in current projections.
9. **The reversibility of ice shelf collapse is uncertain.** Some models say yes, some say no. The physics of vertical mixing at the ice-ocean boundary is not settled.
10. **The US is retreating from Antarctic science** (70.9% proposed cut to polar research) while China and Russia expand (coordinated stations 11 miles apart in Marie Byrd Land).
11. **Private companies are filling the monitoring gap** — and profiting from it. ICEYE ($10B+ valuation) sells data to climate scientists, military/intelligence, and insurance companies.
12. **The krill fishing industry lobbies the body that regulates it.** Aker BioMarine's CEO meets directly with CCAMLR delegations and foreign governments.
13. **Russia maps oil and gas under "science" cover.** The Karpinsky vessel conducts seismic surveys identical to commercial exploration. The Kremlin has allocated 660 billion roubles to natural resource programs including Antarctic geology.
14. **Bioprospecting operates in a legal vacuum.** 200+ patents exist with no clear sovereignty framework. BASF holds 47% of marine genetic patents.
15. **The 2048 mining review is approaching.** The Protocol does not expire, but the review mechanism exists. Russia maps resources, China expands stations, the US cuts science, and seismic companies prepare for "frontier" markets. No explicit industry lobbying for removal of the ban has been documented, but positioning is underway.
16. **No corruption was found.** Despite extensive research into money flows, lobbying, and corporate interests, no corruption scandals, fraud, or financial misconduct in Antarctic research or logistics were identified in 2024–2026.

## 36. What Remains Unknown

1. **What is driving retreat in the northeast Antarctic Peninsula?** Warm water is not the explanation. The mechanism is genuinely unknown.
2. **Was Hektoria's retreat truly unprecedented?** Leading researchers disagree on whether the ice was grounded or floating.
3. **Can ice shelf cavity warming be reversed?** Models conflict. The answer depends on vertical mixing physics that is not fully understood.
4. **How much does biological darkening of ice affect Antarctic melt?** Virtually unstudied compared to Greenland.
5. **How much geothermal heat is beneath Thwaites?** No new measurements since 2014.
6. **Why do some Pacific sectors show cooling or ice gain while the continent overall loses sea ice?** Mechanism unresolved.
7. **Who will win the $8B Antarctic contract?** Protest pending. Expected resolution: September 21, 2026.
8. **What will Aker BioMarine do once private?** No disclosure requirements will apply after August 2026 privatization.
9. **Is China's Antarctic expansion purely scientific?** US intelligence says no. China says yes.
10. **Will the Antarctic mining ban survive beyond 2048?** The treaty does not expire, but the review mechanism exists, and multiple nations are positioning for a potential post-2048 scenario.
